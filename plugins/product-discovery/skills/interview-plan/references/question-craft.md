# Question craft

Read this before writing any line of the guide. Two jobs: kill the questions that produce polite fiction, and stock the guide with probes that produce stories.

## The Mom Test lint

Rob Fitzpatrick's point is not a format, it is a filter: ask in a way that even someone who loves you could not mislead you. Three rules - talk about their life instead of your idea, ask about specifics in the past instead of opinions about the future, talk less and listen more.

Run every question through this table.

| Reject | Why it fails | Rewrite as |
|---|---|---|
| "Would you use X?" | prediction, and people are bad at it | "Tell me about the last time you handled this." |
| "How much would you pay?" | hypothetical money is free | "Walk me through the last invoice you paid for this." |
| "Do you think this is a good idea?" | fishes for a compliment | "Tell me about the last time you had this problem." |
| "Do you struggle with X?" | yes/no, and leading | "Walk me through the last time you did X." |
| "What do you usually do?" | general habit, remembered aspirationally | "Tell me about the most recent time." |
| "Is speed or price more important?" | double-barrelled, forces a false trade | "What made you pick the option you picked last time?" |
| "Our new summary screen shows all of this - useful?" | solution-first, contaminates everything after | ask nothing about the solution until the story is collected |

Rewrite rather than delete. The curiosity behind a bad question is usually right; the phrasing steers the answer.

## Openings that produce a story

- "Tell me about the last time you [behavior]."
- "Walk me through the day you decided to [decision]."
- "Tell me about the most recent time this got hard."

Then excavate. "Last Tuesday after dinner" is not a story yet - ask what happened before it, who else was involved, what they did next.

## Probe library

Adapted from Steve Portigal's seventeen types of interviewing questions. Pick two or three per topic, do not use them all.

**Getting detail**
- Sequence: "What happened first?" Follow with "What happened next?"
- Quantity: "How many times last month?"
- Specific example: "Can you give me a concrete instance?"
- The full list: "What else? ... anything else?" (repeat until they run dry)
- Relationships and structure: "Who else was involved?" Follow with "Who made the final decision?"

**Probing what was not said**
- Clarification: "When you say 'the system', which one do you mean?"
- Native language: "You called it 'the daily panic' - what does that mean here?"
- Emotional cue: "You laughed there. What was that about?"
- Why: "Why that way and not another?"
- Probe without presuming: "How did that affect what happened next?"
- Explain to an outsider: "How would you explain this to a new hire?"

**Contrast, to surface the frame**
- Compare processes: "How is that different from how you did it before?"
- Compare to others, only after the participant mentions a peer: "Walk me through how that person handled it."
- Compare across time: "What changed in the last year?"

**Laddering** - when you need motivation rather than behavior, climb: attribute → functional consequence → personal value. Three "why"s is usually the ceiling before it turns into an interrogation.

**Silence** is a probe. After an answer, wait. The second thing they say is usually more honest than the first.

## The end state

A conversation is worth it if it produced one of three things:

- **Facts** - specific past behavior, with detail.
- **Commitment** - they gave up something they value: time, reputation, money (a follow-up scheduled, data shared, a colleague introduced).
- **Advancement** - they moved a concrete step further in the real process.

Write into the guide which one this study is after and the exact next step the interviewer should ask for. Compliments are not a result.

## Talk ratio

Aim for roughly 20% interviewer, 80% participant. If the guide is dense enough that this is impossible, the guide is too long.
